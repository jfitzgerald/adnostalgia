California Has a Lure that is Stronger than Gold

California has a lure that is stronger than gold - Saturday Evening Post - October 31, 1936

Format
Printed ephemera
Collection
Ad Nostalgia archive

Image note

A streamlined world of motion unfolds across this October 31, 1936, spread from *The Saturday Evening Post*. Beneath a silver airplane, a sleek intercity coach and an open green automobile converge at a monumental highway gateway; a sign points toward Sacramento, San Francisco, and Los Angeles. Warm ochres, deep greens, and the great orange sweep of “California” transform mountains, roadway, and modern transport into a single invitation. The unknown illustrator’s painterly realism is paired with emphatic display lettering and a distinctly streamlined sense of speed and progress.

Yet this advertisement sells more than a western holiday. Placed by *The Saturday Evening Post* itself, it courts California businesses by presenting national advertising as the engine of tourism and commerce. Its copy invokes the state’s celebrated “lure,” stronger than gold, while trains, planes, buses, shops, restaurants, and filling stations promise to convert eastern curiosity into western spending. The intended audience was therefore both the prosperous traveler imagining escape and the advertiser eager to reach that traveler through a mass-circulation magazine.

Published during the Depression, the image offers California as an attainable modern Eden: mobile, sunlit, abundant, and connected. Its confident fantasy leaves economic hardship and social inequality beyond the frame, but captures a powerful interwar ideal—the open road as a route not merely to a destination, but to health, leisure, prosperity, and reinvention.
