Image note

A poised diver arcs across a violet mountain panorama, suspended above a poolside world of palms, striped parasols, swimsuits, and attentive resort guests. The scene appears to evoke Palm Springs and the San Jacinto Mountains, transforming the desert into a theatrical oasis of color and ease. Below, the emphatic red-and-black question—“HOW ABOUT A CHANGE FOR A CHANGE?”—and the broad “SOUTHERN CALIFORNIA” signature anchor the airy illustration with bold display typography. The unnamed artist’s saturated palette and simplified, painterly forms belong to the romantic regionalism of interwar commercial illustration, likely reproduced in color for a national magazine audience.

Issued by the All-Year Club of Southern California, the advertisement invited winter-weary Americans to exchange familiar vacation landscapes for “two other kinds of attractions”: the desert and the Pacific coast. Its copy names Palm Springs, Los Angeles, Long Beach, Pasadena, Beverly Hills, Glendale, Pomona, Santa Monica, and other destinations, while emphasizing accessible travel by train, motor, stage, steamship, or air. Such breadth reflects the region’s growing tourism economy and the expanding culture of long-distance mobility during the 1930s.

The advertisement offered more than climate. It presented Southern California as a modern realm of health, sociability, abundance, and pleasurable reinvention, where seasonal escape could become a way of life. Yet its polished resort idyll—affluent, leisurely, and carefully insulated—leaves outside the frame the labor and unequal access that sustained this sunlit promise.
