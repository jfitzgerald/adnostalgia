California hangs out the welcome sign

Now is the time to plan for your vacation of a lifetime--postwar. And planning is half the fun, especially with the travel picture booklet.

Image note

A jaunty red “C” swings open this invitation to California, where palm trees, surf, and a bathing-suited woman with an upright board transform the state into a sunlit threshold of leisure. Below, Union Pacific maps the journey in bright yellow, red, and black: streamlined trains cross a western route network, while small vignettes promise lounge-car comfort, Boulder Dam, Los Angeles, Las Vegas, and “strange spectaculars.” The lively hand-lettering, simplified figures, and economical washes of color belong to the buoyant tradition of mid-century commercial illustration.

Produced amid wartime anticipation of postwar vacations, the advertisement asks readers to plan for pleasures not yet fully available. California is presented as both industrial powerhouse and welcoming resort, “getting its house in order” for visitors from across the nation. Union Pacific makes mobility itself part of the dream, offering choices from the swift Streamliner to more economical service and turning the rail journey into a “high spot” of the holiday.

Likely intended for a national magazine audience, the page addresses families and middle-class travelers eager for renewed freedom, comfort, and abundance. Its California is an idealized western playground—modern, accessible, and carefree—while the labor, wartime disruption, and social exclusions underlying that promise remain beyond the frame. More than a railroad advertisement, it is a compact vision of postwar optimism: America once again in motion, with California hanging out the welcome sign.
