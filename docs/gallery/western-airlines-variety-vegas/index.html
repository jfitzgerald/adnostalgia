Image note

A slender airliner glides past a towering palm while a collage of picture postcards unfolds below: Lake Mead and Boulder Dam, silhouetted nightclub patrons, horseback riders, neon-lit Fremont Street, and guests basking beside a hotel pool. At the center, the hand-lettered promise “Variety at Vegas” sweeps across a tilted golden card. Saturated desert colors—turquoise water, purple mountains, ocher paper, and vivid reds—give the advertisement the buoyant theatricality of mid-century commercial illustration.

Western Air Lines presents Las Vegas as an accessible wonderland linking Western scenery with metropolitan sophistication. Its Denver–Los Angeles route, with Las Vegas reached in hours, transforms air travel into part of the holiday itself: swift, modern, and liberating. The copy offers fishing and canyon excursions alongside nightclubs, luxury hotels, ranching romance, and swimming pools—an unusually compact inventory of the region’s competing identities. Likely intended for a national magazine or travel publication, the advertisement addresses prosperous leisure travelers drawn to aviation’s expanding reach and to the freedom associated with the American West.

Yet this inviting montage is also selective. The desert appears effortless and abundant, its water, hospitality, and entertainment awaiting the visitor without complication. What Western sold was more than a ticket: a sunlit escape in which Los Angeles, Las Vegas, and the broad Western landscape belonged to one modern geography of pleasure and possibility.
