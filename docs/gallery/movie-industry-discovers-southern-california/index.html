Image note

A jaunty balloon hovers over a Southern California transformed into the whole wide world. Beneath the declaration “THIS LOOKS LIKE A GOOD PLACE,” a teeming pictorial map compresses Alaska, Russia, India, the Alps, the Holy Land, the Arabian Desert, and other screen “locations” into a single sunlit landscape. Saturated blues, ochres, reds, and greens, comic figures, hand-lettered place names, and borders of international costumes give the advertisement the exuberance of an illustrated travel poster. At right, an ornate, theatrical frame announces “1908: Movie Industry discovers Southern California,” linking the region’s scenic variety to Hollywood’s rise.

Issued by the All-Year Club of Southern California, the advertisement likely dates from several decades after the 1908 event it commemorates. Its coupon and offer of an eighty-page guide suggest circulation in a national magazine, where it addressed prospective visitors weighing rail, automobile, bus, steamship, or air travel. Hollywood serves here as both proof and promise: if filmmakers could find the world within a few hours, vacationers might likewise enjoy mountains, beaches, deserts, missions, orange groves, and fashionable resorts in one journey. The playful cartography turns geographic compression into abundance, offering mobility, novelty, and escape. Yet this cosmopolitan dream also reduces distant cultures to picturesque types and leaves outside the frame the region’s unequal histories of land, labor, and representation. As artwork and inducement, it sells not simply a destination, but Southern California as an inexhaustible stage on which any adventure might begin.
