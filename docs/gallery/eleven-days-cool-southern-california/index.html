Image note

A vast, blue Pacific anchors this exuberant invitation to spend “eleven days in cool Southern California” for as little as seventy dollars. Bathers, sailboats, umbrellas, and a palm-lined shore fill the principal illustration, while smaller vignettes promise a Hollywood “first-night,” mountain grandeur, weekly fiestas, and scenic highways for motoring. The composition is both picturesque and practical: loose, painterly color offers romance, while crisp modern lettering, compact descriptive copy, and a mail-in coupon turn the dream into an itinerary. No artist is identified.

Issued in 1931 by the All-Year Club of Southern California, the advertisement promoted the region as a complete vacation world within reach of a two-week holiday. Its emphasis on moderate cost speaks directly to Depression-era travelers, while the automobile, named destinations, and free plan-book suggest an increasingly independent tourist public. The likely audience—middle-class readers of national magazines—was invited to imagine Southern California as healthful, cosmopolitan, and effortlessly varied: ocean bathing in the morning, mountains or motion-picture glamour close at hand.

The advertisement sells more than transportation or lodging; it offers mobility, abundance, and escape under dependable sunshine. Its crowded panorama suppresses the economic hardship and social exclusions of the period, presenting instead a hospitable playground apparently open to all. In that carefully assembled vision, Southern California becomes not merely a place on the map, but a modern way of living—colorful, active, and perpetually on holiday.
